A practical guide to day-to-day healthcare operations

Healthcare administration involves many connected tasks, from verifying insurance coverage to submitting claims and following up on unpaid balances. When these tasks are handled inconsistently, providers may face avoidable rework, delayed payments, and confusion over outstanding claims.

This guide introduces practical ways healthcare teams can organize routine administrative work, identify common billing problems, and maintain clearer records. It is intended for medical practice managers, healthcare administrators, billing staff, and students learning about healthcare operations.

01

Understand the Healthcare Revenue Cycle

See how each administrative stage connects to the next.

The revenue cycle covers the administrative and financial steps involved in receiving payment for healthcare services. Although workflows differ by organization, common stages include:

✓Patient registration and demographic verification
✓Insurance eligibility checks
✓Authorization and referral review, when applicable
✓Clinical documentation and charge capture
✓Medical coding and claim preparation
✓Claim submission and status monitoring
✓Payment posting and reconciliation
✓Denial resolution and accounts receivable follow-up

An error early in the process can create extra work later. For example, an incorrect insurance ID may cause a claim rejection that requires staff to verify the coverage, correct the record, and resubmit the claim.

02

Improve Insurance Verification Before Appointments

Catch information gaps before they create avoidable rework.

Insurance verification helps staff confirm coverage information before services are provided. Verification does not guarantee payment, but it can reveal issues that need attention.

  1. Confirm the patient's demographic information.
  2. Check the member ID and payer details.
  3. Verify coverage for the expected date of service.
  4. Review applicable referral or authorization requirements.
  5. Record the verification date and source.
  6. Explain any unresolved coverage questions to the appropriate staff member.
i
Verification procedures should reflect the payer's rules and the services being provided. Staff should avoid promising coverage or payment based solely on an eligibility response.
03

Keep Provider Enrollment Records Organized

Make application status and next steps easy to identify.

Provider enrollment involves submitting and maintaining information required by government programs, commercial payers, and other healthcare networks. The specific requirements depend on the provider, organization, payer, and jurisdiction.

A central tracking record can include:

✓Provider or organization name
✓Payer or program
✓Application submission date
✓Required documents
✓Current application status
✓Requests for additional information
✓Follow-up dates
✓Confirmed approval and effective dates

Access should be limited to authorized staff, and records should be updated when information changes. Teams should verify current instructions directly with the relevant payer or government program instead of relying on an old checklist.

04

Identify and Address Common Claim Problems

Record the reason, assign the next action, and follow it through.

Claims can be rejected or denied for different reasons. A rejection may indicate that a claim failed an initial submission check, while a denial generally means the payer processed the claim but did not approve payment as submitted. Payer terminology and processes can vary.

Common issues include:

  • Incorrect patient or insurance information
  • Missing or invalid authorization
  • Incomplete documentation
  • Coding or modifier errors
  • Duplicate submissions
  • Filing deadlines that have passed
  • Services that do not meet the payer's coverage requirements

For each issue, record the reason provided, assign responsibility, identify the next action, and document the outcome. Appeals and corrected claims should follow the payer's applicable procedures and deadlines.

05

Monitor Accounts Receivable

Prioritize outstanding balances by risk, reason, and deadline.

Accounts receivable reports show payments that remain outstanding. Reviewing them on a regular schedule helps billing teams prioritize work instead of handling claims solely in the order they appear.

Useful review categories include:

  • Age of the outstanding balance
  • Payer and claim status
  • Amount outstanding
  • Denial or rejection reason
  • Upcoming filing or appeal deadline
  • Last follow-up date
  • Next action and assigned staff member
↗
Older balances may need closer review, but the best priority depends on the reason for nonpayment, the amount at risk, and any relevant deadline. Document follow-up attempts and reconcile payments against payer remittance information.
06

Use Measures That Help Explain Performance

Track a small set of measures using consistent definitions.

A small set of consistently defined measures can help a healthcare organization understand where its revenue cycle needs attention.

MeasureWhat it helps assess
First-pass acceptance rateHow often claims pass initial submission checks
Denial rateHow frequently submitted claims are denied under the chosen definition
Days in accounts receivableHow long balances remain outstanding
Aged receivablesThe portion of balances that have remained unpaid for longer periods
Denial resolution timeHow long it takes to resolve denied claims
Net collection rateCollections compared with the expected collectible amount under the organization's methodology

These measures need consistent definitions and accurate source data. Comparisons are most useful when the reporting period, payer mix, service types, and calculation methods are understood. Avoid treating a single benchmark as suitable for every practice or hospital.

07

Choose Healthcare Revenue Cycle Resources Carefully

Assess fit, responsibilities, reporting, and information safeguards.

Healthcare organizations may need outside support when internal teams lack capacity, billing errors keep recurring, or administrative requirements become difficult to manage. Before choosing a service provider, define the problems that need to be addressed and establish how results will be reviewed.

Questions worth asking include:

  • Which services are included in the agreement?
  • How are claim errors and denials reviewed?
  • What reports will the organization receive?
  • How will responsibilities be divided between the provider and service team?
  • What safeguards protect patient and billing information?
  • How will performance and unresolved issues be communicated?

08

Protect Confidential Healthcare Information

Keep educational resources separate from real operational records.

Billing and enrollment workflows may involve sensitive personal, financial, and medical information. Organizations should use approved systems, restrict access according to job responsibilities, and follow applicable privacy and security requirements.

🔒
Public repositories are suitable for general educational materials, blank templates, and examples using fictional data. They should not contain patient records, real claim documents, credentials, payer portal screenshots containing private information, or confidential business records.

Build consistency into everyday workflows

Reliable healthcare administration depends on accurate information, clear responsibilities, timely follow-up, and consistent review. Teams can begin by documenting their current processes, tracking outstanding enrollment and billing tasks, and investigating recurring errors. Small, well-defined improvements can make problems easier to identify and help staff work from the same information.
